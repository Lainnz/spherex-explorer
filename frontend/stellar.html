      How to read it, and its limits

      Each SPHEREx image sees this spot at a slightly different infrared colour (its filters change colour across the detector), so many images together trace the spectrum. Each channel is a few percent of the wavelength wide (about 0.02 µm at 0.8 µm), so narrow lines show as small bumps, not sharp spikes.

      Limits: measured in a small circle, so close neighbours can blend in; very bright stars overflow the detector (use the light curve's outer-glow option); flagged pixels are left out unless you show them.

            What SPHEREx sees

            SPHEREx is a NASA telescope launched in March 2025. It maps the whole sky in infrared light from 0.75 to 5.0 µm, too red for our eyes, split into 102 colours: a spectrum of every point in the sky.

            • The first moment of the universe: mapping hundreds of millions of galaxies in 3D to look for the pattern left by inflation.
            • Ingredients for life: finding water, CO₂ and CO ice frozen on dust in cold clouds where stars and planets form.
            • All the light galaxies ever made: measuring the faint glow of galaxies too dim to see one by one.

            Rainbow filters. Each of the six detectors sits behind a filter whose colour changes from edge to edge (17 colours each, 6 × 17 = 102). As SPHEREx steps across the sky, a star lands on many colours, and those snapshots build its spectrum (the Spectrum tab).

            Reading the tools

            How often? Most of the sky is seen about every six months. Near the ecliptic poles SPHEREx looks many more times, the best places for the time slider.

            Pixels. One SPHEREx pixel is 6.15″ wide (about 1/300 of the Moon's width). The all-sky map is resampled to about 13″.

            Units. Images are in MJy/sr (brightness per patch of sky). Spectra are in µJy (total light from one source). A jansky is the standard astronomy unit of incoming light.

            Zodiacal light is sunlight scattered by, and heat from, dust in our own solar system. It is the brightest thing SPHEREx sees almost everywhere, so it is modelled and subtracted.

            False colour shows three invisible infrared colours as red, green and blue: a way to see differences, not what your eyes would see.

            Same colour (time bar) plays only frames taken at the same wavelength, and the σ map (Difference) shows which changes are bigger than the noise.
